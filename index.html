About

I am a PhD candidate in Economics at the University of Delaware, specializing in labor and immigration economics, household finance, and shift-share econometrics.

My job market paper studies how immigration affects rents and home values in the United States, how the effects differ by immigrants' educational attainment, and why home values respond more in markets with more restrictive land-use regulation.

Research

Job market paper

Effect of Immigration on the US Housing Market

While a large literature has studied the impact of immigration on the U.S. housing market and found positive effects on rents and home values, this paper adds to it with a heterogeneity analysis by the educational attainment of foreign-born workers, motivated by recent evidence on the role of education in immigrants' upward mobility. Using a share-based approach that draws on recent advances in the shift–share literature, this paper finds that immigration raises rents and home values in the United States, as evident in the Zillow data, and that the home-value response is larger in markets with more restrictive land-use regulation. In addition, the paper presents results using shares from three base years (1980, 1990, and 2000) for all immigrants and for college- and less college-educated immigrants, shedding light on the endogeneity mechanisms behind the shift-share instrument.

Work in progress

Bias–Variance Trade-Offs with Shift-Share Instrumental Variables

Shift-share instruments have become a standard tool for estimating the local effects of immigration, yet researchers must make several design choices when constructing them: whether to use fixed or contemporaneous shares, annual or long differences, and which controls, weights, and samples to apply. This chapter examines how these choices shape the bias–variance tradeoff in shift-share IV designs. Preliminary evidence from the first chapter shows that these choices matter: year-on-year differences produce much stronger and more precise first stages than five-year differences, while contemporaneous shares yield the strongest first stage but raise endogeneity concerns. Building on this evidence, the chapter compares and replicates existing immigration SSIV studies, uses simulations to assess how each choice affects bias and precision, and extends the analysis to ACS data.
